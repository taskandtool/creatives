# Video and UGC hooks and script structures

How the agent opens a video, builds its middle and closes it, and how to read the numbers afterwards. The worked examples use a made-up business, Harlow Joinery, a workshop in Bristol that makes fitted kitchens. Every number in an example is illustrative and must come from the claims ledger before it is used.

Sources: TikTok's own creative guidance (proposition in the first 3 seconds, hook in the first 6, 5 to 10 words of on-screen text a second), Motion's 2025 creative report, Reloop's script-structure list, AdRoast's hook and hold benchmarks. Reloop and AdRoast pages could not be re-fetched on 7 Sep 2026; their structures and benchmarks are taken from the research summary and marked as vendor figures.

## Opening rules

- **No logo in frame one.** The first frame is the hook, on screen and spoken. The name appears after the hook has landed, usually on the lower third at 3 to 6 seconds.
- **No slow establishing shot.** No drone over the city, no pan across the workshop, no title card. Start inside the moment: a hand on the cabinet, a face mid-sentence, the problem already visible.
- **No corporate voice-over.** A person talks to a person. The owner, a fitter, a customer, in their own words and their own accent. If the only voice available is synthetic, keep it in first person and plain, and say so in the brief.
- **Say the proposition by 3 seconds, deliver on the hook by about 6.** If the hook promises "the bit your fitter skips", the viewer sees it by second six.
- **Sound on, 9:16, 15 to 35 seconds** for paid. Captions always; most feed viewers are muted.
- **The first frame doubles as a static.** Whatever is on screen at 0.0 seconds should work as a still with the hook as its headline (see `static-ad/references/formats.md`, First-frame iteration).

## Hook classes

Each class with two written examples. The spoken line and the on-screen text can differ; the on-screen line is usually shorter.

### Call-out / identity
Names the viewer's situation so they know the next 20 seconds are for them. Watch Meta's personal attributes policy: name the house, the job or the situation, never the person's age, health or finances.
1. "If you're renovating a Victorian terrace in Bristol, watch this before you order a kitchen."
2. "Anyone who's had a kitchen fitter say 'the walls aren't straight' and then charge you for it: this is for you."

### Bold claim / contrarian
A statement the viewer does not expect, followed immediately by the reason.
1. "Showroom kitchens are designed for rooms that don't exist."
2. "Eight weeks is the fastest a kitchen should ever be made. Here's why."

### Problem call-out / "nobody tells you"
Names the pain the viewer has felt or will feel.
1. "Nobody tells you the person who measures your kitchen is never the person who fits it."
2. "This 40 mm strip of filler by the fridge is what happens when nobody measured the room."

### Result-first rewind
The payoff is frame one; the story rewinds to how it got there.
1. (Finished kitchen, drawer closing flush) "This drawer closes on a wall that leans 12 mm. Here's how."
2. (Customer in the kitchen) "Ruth got her kitchen on the exact day on the drawing. Let me show you the drawing."

### Specific number / "3 things" / "30 days"
A number that sets an expectation and a length.
1. "Three things I check before I cut a single panel."
2. "Thirty-eight kitchens in 2025. Zero callbacks. Here's the one habit that did it."

### Confession / sceptic
An admission or a doubt, answered.
1. "I'll be honest, eight weeks is a long time to wait for a kitchen."
2. "I didn't think a made-to-fit kitchen was worth the extra. Then I saw the drawings."

### POV / relatable
The viewer's own situation, shown from inside it.
1. (Phone camera on a cabinet door hitting a wall) "POV: the showroom's fitter has just left."
2. (Kitchen table, three quotes laid out) "Three quotes. Three different measurements of the same wall."

### Imperative "stop scrolling"
Rare; use once per campaign at most, and only when the next line earns it.
1. "Stop. If you've paid a kitchen deposit without seeing a drawing, watch this."
2. "Don't order a kitchen this week. Measure your floor first. I'll show you how."

### Controversy
A position some viewers will disagree with, held and explained.
1. "Chipboard carcasses should be illegal in a kitchen."
2. "Most kitchen showrooms don't employ a single joiner."

### Authority explainer
Time in the trade, then the lesson.
1. "Eleven years fitting other people's kitchens taught me one thing."
2. "I've measured about four hundred Bristol kitchens. Almost none of the floors were level."

### Visual pattern interrupts
Openings that stop the thumb with an action rather than a sentence. Pair each with a spoken hook from a class above.
- Reaching into a bag, drawer or fridge and pulling out the subject
- Opening a door into the workshop or the finished room
- Pinch-zoom on a detail (a scribed edge, a filler strip)
- Out-of-box or off-the-bench reveal
- Green-screen walk-in over a screenshot of a bad quote
- Phone dropped onto a worktop, picked up, talk begins
- Megaphone or shouting into an empty kitchen
- Voice-note interface playing over b-roll
- Whiteboard marker starting mid-drawing
Examples: (hand pulls a warped chipboard panel from a skip) "This is what's inside most fitted kitchens." (Pinch-zoom on a drawer front sitting flush) "See that gap? Neither can I."

## Hook stacking

A hook earns about 5 to 15 seconds. Re-hook at every beat boundary: a new claim, a new question, a new visual, a number, a "but". A 30-second cut needs at least three hooks: the opener, one at 8 to 12 seconds, one at 18 to 24 seconds. The re-hook is often the first line of the next beat written as a question or a promise ("Here's the bit nobody checks").

Test 5 to 8 hooks per body. The body is reusable; the first 3 seconds are what gets iterated. Cut the same body with each hook and let the hook rate decide.

## Script the hook and the CTA word for word, the middle as beats

The hook and the call to action are read exactly as written; every word was chosen and will be measured. The middle is written as beats: what is said, what is shown, how long, in a table. The presenter says the beats in their own words. This keeps the parts that matter fixed and the parts that need to sound human loose.

Beat table columns: `t (s)`, `say (beat)`, `show`, `on-screen text`, `re-hook?`.

## The ten script structures

Beat timings for a 15-second and a 30-second cut. The 15-second cut drops the story or the second proof; it never drops the hook or the CTA.

### 1. Testimonial: Hook → Proof → Story → CTA
A customer or a fitter says what happened.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, proof 3 to 8, story 8 to 12, CTA 12 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, proof 3 to 10, story 10 to 24 (re-hook at 12), CTA 24 to 30.
Example hook: "The drawing was the kitchen. Nothing changed on fit day." CTA: "Book a survey at harlowjoinery.co.uk."

### 2. Problem/Solution: Hook → Agitation → Solution → Proof → CTA
The cold default. About 60% of the runtime on the problem and its cost, 40% on the solution.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, agitation 3 to 7, solution 7 to 11, proof 11 to 13, CTA 13 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, agitation 3 to 12 (re-hook at 8), solution 12 to 20, proof 20 to 25, CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "Nobody tells you the person who measures is never the person who fits." CTA: "See how we do it. Link below."

### 3. Comparison: Hook → Option A → Option B → Verdict → CTA
Us against a category, never a named competitor.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, A 3 to 7, B 7 to 11, verdict 11 to 13, CTA 13 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, A 3 to 11, B 11 to 20 (re-hook at 11), verdict 20 to 25, CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "Flat-pack or made-to-fit? Same room, same budget." CTA: "Get the comparison sheet."

### 4. Unboxing / off-the-bench: Hook → Reveal → Detail 1 → Detail 2 → CTA
For a physical product. For a kitchen, the reveal is the finished carcass on the bench or the door opening on fit day.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, reveal 3 to 7, detail 7 to 12, CTA 12 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, reveal 3 to 9, detail 1 9 to 16, detail 2 16 to 24 (re-hook at 16), CTA 24 to 30.
Example hook: "This is what a kitchen looks like before it leaves our workshop." CTA: "Watch the fitting next week. Follow."

### 5. Before/After: Hook → Before → Bridge → After → CTA
Meta restricts this structure for health, weight and skincare; kitchens are fine.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, before 3 to 6, bridge 6 to 9, after 9 to 13, CTA 13 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, before 3 to 10, bridge 10 to 17 (re-hook at 10), after 17 to 25, CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "A 1970s galley in Bishopston. Eight weeks." CTA: "Send us a photo of yours."

### 6. Founder story: Hook → Why I started → The old pain → What we do instead → CTA
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, why 3 to 8, instead 8 to 12, CTA 12 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, why 3 to 10, old pain 10 to 18 (re-hook at 10), instead 18 to 25, CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "Eleven years fitting other people's kitchens taught me one thing." CTA: "If you want yours measured properly, book a survey."

### 7. Tutorial: Hook → Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3 → Result → CTA
Teaches something useful; the product is how the steps get done.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, step 1 3 to 6, step 2 6 to 9, step 3 9 to 12, CTA 12 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, step 1 3 to 10, step 2 10 to 17 (re-hook at 10), step 3 17 to 24 (re-hook at 17), result 24 to 27, CTA 27 to 30.
Example hook: "Three things to check before you pay a kitchen deposit." CTA: "Get the checklist. Link in bio."

### 8. Sceptic: Hook → Doubt → Trial → Surprise → CTA
A customer or the presenter starts unconvinced.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, doubt 3 to 6, trial 6 to 10, surprise 10 to 13, CTA 13 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, doubt 3 to 10, trial 10 to 19 (re-hook at 10), surprise 19 to 25, CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "I didn't think made-to-fit was worth the extra." CTA: "See the drawings we send before a deposit."

### 9. Day in the life: Hook → Morning → The job → The detail → CTA
The workshop or a fit day, shot on a phone.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, morning 3 to 6, job 6 to 11, CTA 11 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, morning 3 to 9, job 9 to 18 (re-hook at 9), detail 18 to 25 (re-hook at 18), CTA 25 to 30.
Example hook: "Fit day in Redland. The wall leans 12 mm and we knew that on Tuesday." CTA: "Follow for the next fit."

### 10. Urgent CTA: Hook → Offer → What's included → Deadline → CTA
Warm and retargeting only; there must be a real deadline or limit in the ledger.
- 15 s: hook 0 to 3, offer 3 to 7, included 7 to 11, deadline and CTA 11 to 15.
- 30 s: hook 0 to 3, offer 3 to 9, included 9 to 18 (re-hook at 9), deadline 18 to 24, CTA 24 to 30.
Example hook: "Four fitting slots left before Christmas." CTA: "Book a survey this week."

## On-screen text rules

- 5 to 10 words a second is the reading limit; a 3-second hook holds at most 12 words on screen.
- Captions for every spoken word, one line at a time, in the safe zone.
- Safe zones for 9:16 (1080 x 1920): keep text out of the top 14% and bottom 35% on Meta Reels and Stories, and out of the top 10%, right 10% and bottom 20% on TikTok. Use each platform's template; the numbers move.
- The hook line is the largest text in the video and sits in the middle third.
- No hashtags on screen. The business name appears once, small, after the hook.
- Text says something the audio does not, or repeats the one line that matters. Do not transcribe everything at display size.

## Metrics and what to change

Definitions:
- **Hook rate** = 3-second video plays ÷ impressions (Meta). TikTok counts 2-second views.
- **Hold rate** = ThruPlays (15 seconds or the whole video) ÷ 3-second plays.
- **Outbound CTR** = link clicks ÷ impressions.

Working targets for a test (vendor figures, unverified): hook rate above 30%, hold rate above 10%, outbound CTR above 1%. Benchmarks quoted by AdRoast and others: Meta hook rate under 25% weak, 25 to 35% solid, 30 to 45% strong; TikTok 30 to 39% competitive, 40% and up elite.

What to change when a number is low:
- **Hook rate low, hold rate fine:** the first 3 seconds. Swap the hook class, change the first frame, add a pattern interrupt, put the on-screen line larger. Keep the body.
- **Hook rate fine, hold rate low:** the hook promised something the body did not deliver by 6 seconds, or there is no re-hook. Move the payoff earlier, cut the establishing beat, add a re-hook at 8 to 12 seconds, shorten the cut.
- **Both fine, CTR low:** the CTA or the offer. Make the CTA a verb with an expectation ("Book a survey, we come out within a week"), put it on screen as well as spoken, and check the landing page says the same thing as the hook.
- **CTR fine, conversions low:** not a creative problem. Check the landing page, the form and the offer before touching the video.
- **Everything fine, then falling:** fatigue. See refresh triggers in `angles/references/strategy.md`.

## No footage: talking head vs b-roll vs slideshow

For a business with no video, in order of preference:
- **Talking head** if the owner or a fitter will speak to a phone for 30 seconds. Phone at eye level, window light, one take per hook, captions added. It beats everything else for a service business because the person is the proof.
- **B-roll with voice** if they will not be on camera but will record audio or let the agent use a first-person script. Phone clips of the workshop, the bench, the van, a fit in progress; 2 to 4 second cuts; the spoken hook over the first clip.
- **Slideshow** if there is nothing but photos. Five to eight stills, 2 to 3 seconds each, the hook on the first, one line of text per still, a customer quote as one of the stills, CTA on the last. Use before/after pairs and job photos, not stock. It is the weakest of the three but a first-frame static from it still tests the hook.
Do not use stock footage of a kitchen the business did not make or a generic actor as "the owner"; both are a claim the ledger cannot support.
